What it is

Airspace you can actually read

Aeronautical charts pack airspace into flat lines and symbols, and the layers stack: a Class C shelf might begin 2,500 feet up, with another above it and another above that. For a paraglider or drone pilot the real question is simpler. Can I fly here, and how high?

Airplan draws Canada’s airspace on a topographic map. Click a point and it lists every layer above it, with floors and ceilings in feet and metres, then shows the whole stack in 3D.

Why it’s useful

The answer, not the chart

  • One click, the whole column

    No decoding symbols or cross-referencing supplements. Tap the map and every layer above that point is listed in order.

  • Feet and metres side by side

    Airspace is defined in feet; free-flight pilots and drone operators often think in metres. Airplan labels every boundary in both.

  • Terrain in the picture

    The topographic base map puts airspace floors next to the mountains, which matters when launch is already 1,500 m up.

  • Made for free flight

    Thermal overlays for soaring pilots, and Liftseeker links straight to the airspace above each launch.

How it works

From a click to a flight plan

  1. 01

    See the stack in 3D

    Each layer becomes a coloured cylinder at its real floor and ceiling, so it’s obvious where the gaps are and how much room you have underneath.

  2. 02

    Plan a route

    Draw a route, or import a GPX or KML track, and Airplan checks the airspace and terrain along a corridor on either side of it, with distance markers as you go.

  3. 03

    Show only what matters

    Pick from fifteen base maps, add thermal overlays, and hide the airspace classes or altitude bands that don’t apply to how you fly.

Under the hood

How it’s built

Map
Leaflet, on OpenTopoMap by default
3D
Three.js through React Three Fiber
Airspace
OpenAir definitions parsed into polygons, with a point-in-polygon lookup for every click
Framework
Next.js, hosted on Vercel
